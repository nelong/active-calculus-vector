After doing the preview activity and looking at Figure 11.10.1, you may be wondering if there are other contours that intersect the constraint line and have a point where the gradient to the objective function and the gradient to the constraint function are parallel. Fortunately, we can compute partial derivatives to understand the gradients and our algebraic understanding of what it means for two vectors to be parallel to determine that there are no other points. Summarizing the information from the Preview Activity, we define
\begin{align*} f(x,y) \amp= x^2 y \\ g(x,y) \amp= 4x+y \end{align*}
and wish to maximize \(f\) subject to the constraint \(g(x,y) = 12\text{.}\) Computing the gradients gives us
\begin{align*} \nabla f(x,y) \amp = \langle 2xy, x^2\rangle\\ \nabla g(x,y) \amp = \langle 4,1 \rangle\text{.} \end{align*}
Now recall that two vectors are parallel exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other. Algebraically, this happens when one vector is a scalar multiple of the other:
\begin{equation} \nabla f = \lambda g\text{.}\tag{11.10.1} \end{equation}
We refer to the scalar \(\lambda\) as a Lagrange multiplier. For our objective and constraint functions, this gives rise to the system of equations:
\begin{align} 2xy\amp = 4\lambda\tag{11.10.2}\\ x^2\amp = \lambda\tag{11.10.3}\\ 4x+y\amp = 12 \tag{11.10.4} \end{align}
The first two equations come from equating the first and second components of (11.10.1), respectively. The third equation is the constraint equation.
