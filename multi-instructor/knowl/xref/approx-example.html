Example 11.6.6.

Suppose we have a machine that cuts rectangles of width \(w=20\) cm and height \(h=10\) cm. However, the machine isn’t perfect, and therefore the width could be off by \(dw = \Delta w = 0.2\) cm and the height could be off by \(dh = \Delta h = 0.4\) cm.
🔗
The area of the rectangle is
\begin{equation*} A(w,h) = wh, \end{equation*}
which means that the area of a perfectly-manufactured rectangle is \(A(20, 10) = 200\) square centimeters. Since the machine isn’t perfect, we would like to know how much the area of a given rectangle could differ from the perfect rectangle. We will estimate the uncertainty in the area using steps in each variable along the linearization to estimate the change in the area. In particular, this looks like
\begin{equation*} \Delta A \approx A_w(20, 10) \enspace \Delta w + A_h(20,10) \enspace \Delta h \end{equation*}
🔗
By computing partial derivatives, we find that \(A_w = h\) and \(A_h = w\text{.}\) This implies that
\begin{equation*} \Delta A \approx 10 \enspace \Delta w + 20 \enspace \Delta h = 10 (0.2) + 20 (0.4) = 10\text{.} \end{equation*}
That is, we estimate that the area in our rectangles could be off by as much as 10 square centimeters.
