    Section 12.7 Triple Integrals in Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

    Motivating Questions

    • What is the volume element in cylindrical coordinates and how do we use this to evaluate a triple integral as an iterated integral in cylindrical coordinates?
      🔗
      🔗
    • What is the volume element in spherical coordinates and how do we use this to evaluate a triple integral as an iterated integral in spherical coordinates?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 12.7.1 Introduction

    In Section 12.5, we how polar coordinates can provide a simpler way to write iterated integrals when trying to evaluate a double integral. Double integrals with integrands and regions of integration that are simple to write in polar coordinates are the most convenient to convert, as long as care is taken with the area element, which is \(dA = r\, dr \, d\theta\) when using polar coordinates.
    🔗
    In Section 9.8, we introduced the cylindrical and spherical coordinate systems and explored converting points, equations, and regions between these and rectangular coordinate systems in three dimensions. Cylindrical coordinates are most commonly used in situations with rotational symmetry around the \(z\)-axis, while spherical coordinates are primarily used when there is rotational symmetry around the origin. You may find it useful to review the conversion equations between cylindrical and rectangular coordinates and conversion equations between spherical and rectangular coordinates. This section’s Preview Activity asks you to think about some common surfaces in cylindrical or spherical coordinates before starting to use them for triple integrals.
    🔗

    In this section, we will look at converting triple integrals to use cylindrical and spherical coordinates and similar to our approach in Section 12.5. Just as with writing a double integral in polar coordinates, there are three essential steps to writing a triple integral in cylindrical or spherical coordinates:
    1. Convert the volume element \(dV\) to the new coordinate system.
      🔗
    2. Describe the region of integration by using inqualities involving the variables of the new coordinate system.
      🔗
    3. Write the integrand \(f(x,y,z)\) using the new coordinate system.
      🔗
    We begin with cylindrical coordinates, for which we can rely on much of what we have already discussed about polar coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 12.7.2 Triple Integrals in Cylindrical Coordinates

    To evaluate a triple integral \(\iiint_S f(x,y,z) \, dV\) as an iterated integral in rectangular coordinates, we use the fact that the volume element \(dV\) is equal to \(dz \, dy \, dx\text{,}\) corresponding to the volume of a small box. To evaluate a triple integral in cylindrical coordinates, we similarly must understand the volume element \(dV\) in cylindrical coordinates.
    🔗
    In particular, we consider the volume of a solid created by a small step in each of the coordinates: \(\Delta r\text{,}\) \(\Delta \theta\text{,}\) \(\Delta z\text{.}\) Figure 12.7.1 shows an example of such a solid. You should recognize the shaded area in the \(xy\)-plane as the area element used for double integrals in polar coordinates. Recall that \(\Delta A\) was \(\frac{1}{2}(r_{i+1}+r_i) \Delta r \ \Delta \theta\text{.}\) Since \(z\) is the perpendicular height of our solid, its volume is the product of the base area and the height:
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V = \Delta A \ \Delta z = \frac{(r_{i+1}+r_i)}{2} \Delta r \, \Delta \theta \, \Delta z \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Remember that as \(\Delta r\) shrinks, \(\frac{(r_{i+1}+r_i)}{2}\) approaches the \(r\)-coordinate of this solid. Therefore the volume element \(dV\) in cylindrical coordinates is \(dV = r \, dz \, dr \, d\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because we can think of cylindrical coordinates as polar coordinates plus \(z\text{,}\) slicing the projection of a solid onto the \(xy\)-plane into a radially simple or angularly simple description provides two of the bounds of an iterated integral used to evaluate the triple integral. Completing the description requires also providing inequalities for \(z\text{.}\) Finally, since the \(z\)-coordinate in cylindrical coordinates is the same as in rectangular coordinates, writing the integrand in cylindrical coordinates requires only converting the first two coordinates to polar coordinates using the equations \(x=r\cos(\theta)\) and \(y=r\sin(\theta)\text{.}\) We summarize this below.
    🔗

    Triple integrals in cylindrical coordinates.

    Given a continuous function \(f = f(x,y,z)\) over a region \(S\) in \(\R^3\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_S f(x,y,z) \, dV = \iiint_S f(r\cos(\theta), r\sin(\theta), z) \, r \, dz \, dr \, d\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The latter expression is an iterated integral in cylindrical coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗
    While the volume element and the conversion of the integrand are done exactly the same for every triple integral written in cylindrical coordinates, the task of writing an iterated integral in cylindrical coordinates requires us to slice the particular region in terms of simple bounds on \(\theta\text{,}\) \(r\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) In the following activity, we explore how to do this in several situations where cylindrical coordinates are natural and advantageous. This can be both in terms of providing simple expressions for the bounds on the variables and providing an integrand that is easier to integrate than the rectangular coodinate version would be. In most of the activities in this section, we ask you to focus on setting up the integral in the given coordinate system and do not ask you to evaluate the integral. Most of the insight in working in cylindrical and spherical coordinates comes from setting up the integrals. Once you have done that, you should be able to recognize how you would be able to evaluate the integral with a modest amount of work, but we will usually direct you to not complete that evaluation.

    Subsection 12.7.3 Triple Integrals in Spherical Coordinates

    Similar to rectangular and cylindrical coordinates, when working in spherical coordinates we usually write \(\rho\) as a function of \(\theta\) and \(\phi\text{.}\) This is a natural analog to polar coordinates, where the distance from the origin in the plane is often expressed as a function of \(\theta\text{.}\) In spherical coordinates, writing \(\rho\) as a function of \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) allows us to represent distance from the origin as a function of two key angles.
    🔗
    A triple integral \(\iiint_S f(x,y,z) \, dV\) in spherical coordinates can be evaluated as an iterated integral once we understand the volume element \(dV\text{.}\) Figure 12.7.2 illustrates a spherical “box” generated by a small change in each of the \(\rho\text{,}\) \(\theta\text{,}\) and \(\phi\) coordinates. A conceptual description for where the volume element comes from is given in the proof to Key Idea 12.7.3.
    🔗

    Key Idea 12.7.3. Triple integrals in spherical coordinates.

    The volume element for spherical coordinates is \(dV= \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \, d\rho \, d\theta \, d\phi\text{.}\) Given a continuous function \(f = f(x,y,z)\) over a region \(S\) in \(\R^3\text{,}\) the triple integral \(\iiint_S f(x,y,z) \, dV\) can be written in spherical coordinates as
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_S f(\rho\sin(\phi)\cos(\theta), \rho \sin(\phi) \sin(\theta), \rho \cos(\phi)) \, \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \, d\rho \, d\theta \, d\phi \text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The latter expression is an iterated integral in spherical coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    To find the volume element \(dV\) in spherical coordinates, we must understand how to determine the volume of a spherical box of the form \(\rho_1 \leq \rho \leq \rho_2\) (with \(\Delta \rho = \rho_2-\rho_1)\text{,}\) \(\phi_1 \leq \phi \leq \phi_2\) (with \(\Delta \phi = \phi_2-\phi_1\)), and \(\theta_1 \leq \theta \leq \theta_2\) (with \(\Delta \theta = \theta_2-\theta_1\)). An illustration of such a box is given in Figure 12.7.2. This spherical box is more complicated than the cylindrical box we encountered earlier. Rather than computing the volume \(\Delta V\)directly, we will focus on approximating it. We can approximate the volume \(\Delta V\) of this spherical box by the volume of a box in rectangular coordinates whose sides have the lengths of the sides of the spherical box. The three sides we will use are highlighted in the figure.
    🔗
    Along the red side, \(\rho\) changes while \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) are constant. The length of the red side is simply the change in \(\rho\text{,}\) as indicated by the label \(\Delta \rho\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Along the green side, \(\phi\) changes while \(\theta\) and \(\rho\) are constant. To focus our analysis, we plot only the view looking along the plane \(\theta=\theta_0\) in Figure 12.7.4.
    🔗
    This is the same kind of picture as we used when looking at the area element in polar coordinates. The length of the green curve labeled \(s_1\) is \(\rho_1 \Delta\phi\) by the same type of argument used in polar coordinates:
    \begin{equation*} (\text{fraction of circle})(\text{circumference of circle})=\left(\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi}\right)\left(2\pi \rho_1 \right)= \rho_1 \Delta\phi \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Along the blue side, \(\theta\) changes while \(\rho\) and \(\phi\) are constant. In other words, the blue curve consists of \(x=\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0) \cos(\theta), y= \rho_1 \sin(\phi_0), z=\rho_1 \cos(\phi_0)\) which corresponds to a circle of radius \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0)\) parallel to the \(xy\)-plane and positioned at \(z=\rho_1 \cos(\phi_0)\text{.}\) We can use the same type of argument as above that arc length of a curve corresponding to an angular change of \(\Delta \theta\) along a circle of radius \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0)\) is \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0) \Delta \theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Remember that for very small changes in the coordinates, the difference between the values of the coordinates used shrinks. For example, we are in the setting where \(\rho=0 \approx \rho_1 \approx \rho\text{.}\) By multiplying the lengths of the sides of the spherical box, we obtain an approximation of its volume:
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx \left(\Delta\rho\right) \left(\rho \, \Delta\phi \right)\left(\rho \sin(\phi) \, \Delta\theta \right) = \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \Delta\phi \, \Delta\theta \, \Delta\rho \end{equation*}
    This leads us to the volume element in spherical coordinates
    \begin{equation*} dV = \rho^2\sin(\phi)\, d\phi\, d\theta\, d\rho\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The conversion of the integrand is straightforward because \(x= \rho\sin(\phi)\cos(\theta), y=\rho \sin(\phi) \sin(\theta), z=\rho \cos(\phi) \) does not require interpretation based on the coordinates used.
    🔗
    🔗
    To complete the setup of an iterated integral in spherical coordinates, we must determine the limits of integration in \(\phi\text{,}\) \(\theta\text{,}\) and \(\rho\text{.}\) The process is similar to other coordinate systems. The central idea in this process will be to fix all but one coordinate and give the same bounding functions for the remaining coordinate, then project onto the remaining coordinates and repeat this process.
