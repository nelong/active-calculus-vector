Key Idea 12.7.3. Triple integrals in spherical coordinates.

The volume element for spherical coordinates is \(dV= \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \, d\rho \, d\theta \, d\phi\text{.}\) Given a continuous function \(f = f(x,y,z)\) over a region \(S\) in \(\R^3\text{,}\) the triple integral \(\iiint_S f(x,y,z) \, dV\) can be written in spherical coordinates as
\begin{equation*} \iiint_S f(\rho\sin(\phi)\cos(\theta), \rho \sin(\phi) \sin(\theta), \rho \cos(\phi)) \, \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \, d\rho \, d\theta \, d\phi \text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
The latter expression is an iterated integral in spherical coordinates.
🔗
🔗

Proof.
To find the volume element \(dV\) in spherical coordinates, we must understand how to determine the volume of a spherical box of the form \(\rho_1 \leq \rho \leq \rho_2\) (with \(\Delta \rho = \rho_2-\rho_1)\text{,}\) \(\phi_1 \leq \phi \leq \phi_2\) (with \(\Delta \phi = \phi_2-\phi_1\)), and \(\theta_1 \leq \theta \leq \theta_2\) (with \(\Delta \theta = \theta_2-\theta_1\)). An illustration of such a box is given in Figure 12.7.2. This spherical box is more complicated than the cylindrical box we encountered earlier. Rather than computing the volume \(\Delta V\)directly, we will focus on approximating it. We can approximate the volume \(\Delta V\) of this spherical box by the volume of a box in rectangular coordinates whose sides have the lengths of the sides of the spherical box. The three sides we will use are highlighted in the figure.
🔗
Along the red side, \(\rho\) changes while \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) are constant. The length of the red side is simply the change in \(\rho\text{,}\) as indicated by the label \(\Delta \rho\text{.}\)
🔗
Along the green side, \(\phi\) changes while \(\theta\) and \(\rho\) are constant. To focus our analysis, we plot only the view looking along the plane \(\theta=\theta_0\) in Figure 12.7.4.
🔗
This is the same kind of picture as we used when looking at the area element in polar coordinates. The length of the green curve labeled \(s_1\) is \(\rho_1 \Delta\phi\) by the same type of argument used in polar coordinates:
\begin{equation*} (\text{fraction of circle})(\text{circumference of circle})=\left(\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi}\right)\left(2\pi \rho_1 \right)= \rho_1 \Delta\phi \end{equation*}
🔗
Along the blue side, \(\theta\) changes while \(\rho\) and \(\phi\) are constant. In other words, the blue curve consists of \(x=\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0) \cos(\theta), y= \rho_1 \sin(\phi_0), z=\rho_1 \cos(\phi_0)\) which corresponds to a circle of radius \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0)\) parallel to the \(xy\)-plane and positioned at \(z=\rho_1 \cos(\phi_0)\text{.}\) We can use the same type of argument as above that arc length of a curve corresponding to an angular change of \(\Delta \theta\) along a circle of radius \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0)\) is \(\rho_1 \sin(\phi_0) \Delta \theta\text{.}\)
🔗
Remember that for very small changes in the coordinates, the difference between the values of the coordinates used shrinks. For example, we are in the setting where \(\rho=0 \approx \rho_1 \approx \rho\text{.}\) By multiplying the lengths of the sides of the spherical box, we obtain an approximation of its volume:
\begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx \left(\Delta\rho\right) \left(\rho \, \Delta\phi \right)\left(\rho \sin(\phi) \, \Delta\theta \right) = \rho^2 \sin(\phi) \Delta\phi \, \Delta\theta \, \Delta\rho \end{equation*}
This leads us to the volume element in spherical coordinates
\begin{equation*} dV = \rho^2\sin(\phi)\, d\phi\, d\theta\, d\rho\text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
The conversion of the integrand is straightforward because \(x= \rho\sin(\phi)\cos(\theta), y=\rho \sin(\phi) \sin(\theta), z=\rho \cos(\phi) \) does not require interpretation based on the coordinates used.
